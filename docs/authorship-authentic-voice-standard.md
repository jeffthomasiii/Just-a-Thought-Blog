# Just A Thought — Authorship & Authentic Voice Standard

**Status:** Authoritative Editorial Standard  
**Adopted:** 2026-08-10  
**Updated:** 2026-08-11  
**Applies to:** JAT brainstorming, research, drafting, revision, copy editing, guest and contributor content, and archive remediation

## 1. Purpose

**Just A Thought is primarily authored by Jeff Thomas III and may also publish clearly attributed guest and contributor work.** Jeff-authored articles begin with his thoughts, questions, observations, experiences, study, convictions, relationships, source material, or ongoing reflection. Guest and contributor work must likewise remain attributable to the person whose thought, experience, study, or perspective is being published.

This standard exists to protect that human origin as a thought moves from rough material to a finished article. The goal is not merely to preserve a recognizable writing style. It is to preserve the actual process of reflection: where the thought came from, what raised the question, what shaped it, what remains uncertain, and where the author has honestly arrived.

Editing, research assistance, reference materials, conversations, software, and other collaborative tools may help develop or refine a piece. They must not replace the real catalyst, personal material, judgment, theological care, or conclusions that make the article the author's.

When choosing between a cleaner sentence and a more authentic one, preserve authenticity unless clarity genuinely suffers. When choosing between a stronger claim and a more accurate one, choose accuracy. When genuine uncertainty exists, represent it honestly.

## 2. The JAT Fingerprint

A recurring JAT movement is:

> **event/catalyst → observation → question → processing → Scripture, experience, person, or source enters the conversation → thought develops → provisional reflection**

Use this movement when it naturally fits. It is a fingerprint, not a mandatory template.

Before drafting, identify why the thought exists. The catalyst may be something the author experienced, a conversation, something a pastor or another person said, a passage of Scripture, a book or podcast, work, marriage, technology, culture, camping, creation, or an unresolved question.

Do not manufacture a personal hook when none exists.

## 3. Preserve Before Replacing

An author's phrases, dictated thoughts, observations, analogies, questions, rough prose, stories, and distinctive wording are source material.

When revising:

- preserve recognizable language when it communicates the thought clearly;
- improve grammar, clarity, pacing, and flow without erasing useful irregularity;
- do not replace specific language with generic “better writing” merely because it sounds more polished;
- do not invent transitions that imply experiences, emotions, motives, or conclusions the author did not provide;
- recover the authentic voice already present in older articles rather than making the archive sound uniformly contemporary.

For Jeff-authored work, preserve Jeff's established JAT voice. For guest and contributor work, do not rewrite the contributor into Jeff's voice merely to make the article sound like the rest of JAT.

## 4. Reflection Mode and Teaching Mode

### Reflection Mode — default

Most JAT writing should allow readers to see the thought developing. Reflection Mode invites the reader to think alongside the author rather than simply receive conclusions.

It can include uncertainty, questions, tension, changing understanding, relational specificity, and a conclusion that remains appropriately open-handed.

### Teaching Mode — when the subject requires it

Teaching Mode is appropriate for exegesis, theology, instruction, historical explanation, technical clarification, or an argument that genuinely requires structured explanation.

Even in Teaching Mode, do not manufacture certainty, flatten complexity, or turn the prose into a sermon outline unless the content actually calls for that form.

## 5. Human Anchors

Preserve genuine anchors already present in the source material.

### Experience

Concrete events, work, marriage, ministry, travel, camping, conversations, reading, or ordinary life that actually prompted or shaped the thought.

### Vulnerability

Real uncertainty, limitation, tension, correction, or self-examination that the author has actually expressed. Never manufacture vulnerability for emotional effect.

### Detail

Specific people, places, circumstances, sources, and observations that make the thought traceable to a real origin.

### Humility

Language that accurately represents the strength of the evidence, Scripture, experience, or conclusion.

### Curiosity

Questions that genuinely arise from the thought rather than rhetorical questions added merely for style.

### Relational Specificity

When a spouse, pastor, friend, coworker, family member, church, sermon, author, book, podcast, or other source materially shaped the thought, preserve that provenance when appropriate.

## 6. Patterns to Watch

### Fragmentation

Avoid stacking dramatic one-sentence paragraphs or sentence fragments simply to create emphasis.

### Symmetry

Avoid forcing ideas into threes, balanced contrasts, equal sections, repeated sentence patterns, or perfectly parallel constructions when the thought did not naturally develop that way.

### Genericity

Watch for language that could belong to almost any Christian article, leadership post, devotional, or motivational essay. Return to the specific catalyst, question, Scripture, relationship, or tension.

### Excessive Polish

Do not optimize every sentence into a maxim, quotation, or social-media-ready line. Natural prose may contain longer sentences, uneven paragraph lengths, qualifications, and ordinary connective language.

### Template Visibility

Structure should serve the thought. Do not make every article visibly follow the same Hook → Three Points → Lesson → Challenge pattern.

### Overstatement

Do not strengthen a claim beyond what Scripture, evidence, experience, or a source establishes.

## 7. Natural Prose

Write paragraphs that develop complete thoughts. Most paragraphs should contain roughly three to six sentences unless a shorter paragraph genuinely serves the narrative.

Allow natural variation in paragraph and section length. Emphasis should come primarily from the progression of ideas rather than fragments, rhetorical triples, repeated contrasts, or dramatic formatting.

JAT should read like a thoughtful essay or conversation, not a motivational speech, sermon outline, LinkedIn post, or generic devotional.

## 8. Scripture, Truth, and Theological Care

Scripture must shape the thought rather than merely support a predetermined conclusion. JAT's willingness to wrestle openly with questions and ideas does not imply that truth is relative, that contradictory conclusions are equally sound, or that sincerity makes an interpretation true. Scripture remains the governing authority for biblical and theological claims.

Clearly distinguish:

- exegesis from application;
- application from analogy;
- theological synthesis from explicit biblical statements;
- observation, inference, belief, and speculation from biblical authority.

Do not proof-text, assign unsupported motives to God or biblical characters, overstate Greek or Hebrew word meanings, or present a modern framework as though it were the meaning of a passage.

When Scripture is explicit, definitive language may be appropriate. When the conclusion is interpretive, inferential, or personal, use language that reflects that level of certainty. Humility means representing the strength of a conclusion accurately, not treating every possible conclusion as equally valid.

## 9. Sources and Provenance

When a sermon, pastor, book, author, conversation, podcast, article, cultural event, work experience, Scripture reading, or other source materially shaped the thought, preserve that provenance when appropriate.

Distinguish what the source establishes from the author's application, interpretation, synthesis, or reflection.

Do not absorb another person's idea into JAT prose in a way that makes it appear to have originated with the credited author.

## 10. Guest Writers, Contributors, and Collaborators

JAT may include guest writers, contributors, pastors, friends, interview participants, or other collaborators whose perspectives add something meaningful to the conversation.

- Clearly identify the author or contributor responsible for the perspective being published.
- Preserve the contributor's own voice rather than making every contribution sound as though Jeff wrote it.
- Do not assume that one contributor's conclusion represents Jeff, another contributor, or a universal JAT position merely because the work appears on the site.
- Publication is an invitation to thoughtful engagement, not a declaration that every statement by every contributor carries equal authority or is beyond examination.
- Contributor work should remain consistent with JAT's commitment to Scripture, thoughtful reflection, humility, accuracy, and care for people.

JAT is a place where authors and readers may wrestle honestly with Scripture, experience, questions, and ideas. That wrestling is directed toward truth rather than away from it. The aim is not to create a space where every conclusion is treated as equally valid, but one where claims can be examined carefully, interpretations can be distinguished from what Scripture explicitly says, and people can remain teachable as their understanding develops.

## 11. Personal Material

Never invent an author's experiences, conversations, feelings, memories, beliefs, vulnerability, spiritual experiences, motivations, or conclusions.

Do not infer a personal reaction merely because it would make a stronger article. If a personal detail is needed but not available, leave the gap visible, write around it, or ask for it during collaboration.

Do not artificially “humanize” prose through fake anecdotes, invented dialogue, deliberate errors, slang, or cosmetic imperfection.

## 12. Structure

The traditional JAT structure remains a guide:

> Hook/Catalyst → Context & Reflection → Development → Resolution/Open-Ended Reflection → Signature Closing → Optional Reader Prompt

But structure follows the thought. Articles do not need three points, equal-length sections, a resolved lesson, or a reader prompt.

Most Jeff-authored reflections should end with:

> “…just a thought.”

Exceptions include guest contributors, formal resources rather than reflections, or situations where pastoral sensitivity calls for another ending.

## 13. Editorial Assistance and Tools

JAT may use research tools, reference materials, editorial feedback, software, and AI-assisted tools during development and revision. Their role is supportive: helping explore questions, locate or compare sources, organize material, test structure, identify clarity problems, or consider alternate wording.

When AI is used, it functions as an editorial or research assistant working from the author's actual thought and source material. It must not invent the experience, conviction, memory, relationship, spiritual claim, or personal conclusion behind an article.

The same standards apply regardless of the tool being used: preserve provenance, distinguish research from interpretation, do not manufacture personal material, and do not strengthen claims beyond their support.

## 14. Working by Stage

### Brainstorming

Surface questions, tensions, related Scriptures, possible structures, research needs, and alternate ways of approaching the thought without deciding what the author believes.

### Research

Locate, compare, summarize, and organize useful sources. Keep source-derived claims distinguishable from the author's interpretation or application.

### Draft Development

Develop the article from the actual catalyst and supplied material. Preserve the author's language, provenance, specificity, questions, and uncertainty. Do not fill personal gaps with plausible invention.

### Revision

Preserve before replacing. Improve clarity and flow while protecting distinctive language, relational detail, uncertainty, and the visible processing of the thought.

### Archive Remediation

Evaluate older writing in its own context. Correct weak structure, clarity issues, metadata problems, or conspicuous formulaic patterns without rewriting the archive into a single modern voice.

## 15. Article Length

Treat length as an editorial range, not a quota:

- Brief reflections/devotionals: **700–1,100 words**
- Standard articles: **1,200–1,800 words**
- Series/deeper theological reflections: **1,600–2,200 words**
- Cornerstone resources/guides: **2,200–3,500 words**

Most future articles should fall between **1,200–2,000 words**. Never pad or compress a thought merely to meet a range.

## 16. Final JAT Check

Before substantial writing is considered complete, ask:

1. Why does this article exist?
2. Can the reader see the author processing the thought?
3. Is genuine provenance and specificity preserved?
4. Does it sound recognizably like the credited author while remaining appropriate for JAT?
5. Does Scripture shape rather than decorate the reflection?
6. Are interpretation and certainty represented accurately?
7. Have any claims become stronger than their support?
8. Are fragmentation, symmetry, genericity, excessive polish, template visibility, or overstatement becoming conspicuous?
9. Is authorship and contributor attribution clear?
10. Does the ending honestly reflect where the thought arrived?

If the article passes those checks, do not keep polishing merely for the sake of polish.
